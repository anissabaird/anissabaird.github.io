Welcome!
======
I am a PhD candidate in the department of Linguistics at the University of Toronto. My research interests are predominantly in sociophonetics and queer linguistics, although I've also been known to work in psycholinguistics (namely, child language acquisition) and heritage languages.

My ongoing work focuses on the relationship between linguistic variation and identity construction by exploring how nonbinary individuals in the Greater Toronto Area (GTA) utilize linguistic style-shifting to construct and negotiate their nonbinary identities. By integrating experimental and interactional approaches, I investigate not only the perceptions of nonbinary linguistic styles and their social/indexical meanings, but also the production of these styles as a form of stance-taking.  In addition, I look at how this production is affected by similar or differing gender ideologies (particularly with regard to the "gendered voice") that exist with their conversational partner. My main goals in this research are threefold: (1) To contribute to the theory of style by looking at how social meaning is shaped by the interplay of perception and production, (2) To contribute to both queer and trans linguistic theory by both focusing on social meanings that contribute to gender identity construction as they arise from local interaction, as well as by working against the homogenization of nonbinary social practice into a deterministic category, and (3) To give space for nonbinary individuals to share their experiences and perspectives, thereby helping to create and maintain a safe space in linguistic research for educating and embracing variation in how nonbinary individuals outwardly present their equally varied identities. 

Related to queer linguistics, I also look at how children interpret/comprehend ambiguous pronouns that are not strictly associated with binary gender (e.g., singular *they*), and how this compares to adult individuals. Additionally, I am interested in the portrayals of gender nonconforming characters in fictional media (e.g., video games) and how the voices of these characters are constructed in order to index (or avoid indexing) gender nonconformity. Previous work of mine has also focused on how nonbinary individuals may or may not uniquely make use of a phenomenon known as phonetic convergence: a form of phonetic imitation where conversational partners begin to sound more (or sometimes less!) phonetically similar to each other.
